# From a Sketch to a Roller Coaster

This folder is the complete delivery package for the Lucas Academy Golden
Funnel video. It follows one real creative process: a child asks a question,
shares a pencil sketch, improves the idea through conversation, turns it into
one mathematical Surface, builds it in the museum, and finally rides it into a
black hole.

All visible text and narration are in English. The mathematics may look
advanced, but the story is written so a child around age ten to twelve can
follow the main idea: **ask, draw, make a rule, test it, and ride it.**

## Final videos

- `final-long.mp4` — the complete 16:9 film, 1920 × 1080, 60 fps, 94.05 seconds.
- `final-short-vertical.mp4` — the complete 9:16 social cut, 1080 × 1920,
  60 fps, 59.78 seconds. Milestones 1, 2, and the Agent build are composed
  natively for portrait. Only the real horizontal gameplay is preserved inside
  a vertical presentation instead of being cropped.

The three approved long-form milestone sources are in `clips/`:

1. `01-milestone-1.mp4` — the first questions and the hand-drawn spiral.
2. `02-milestone-2.mp4` — the spiral becomes one 3D Surface.
3. `03-milestone-3-build.mp4` — code, tests, deployment, and `READY TO RIDE`.

Every act change uses a short fade through black. The gameplay source has no
audio, so the final ride is intentionally quiet unless music or sound design is
added later.

## Child-friendly English voice-over

The time ranges below follow `final-long.mp4`. Read warmly and curiously at
about 125–140 words per minute. Leave small gaps so the drawing, formula, word
cloud, and ride can speak for themselves.

### 0:00–0:36 — Milestone 1: a question becomes a sketch

> Can a little pencil spiral become a real roller coaster?

> I did not begin with the answer. I began with a question.

> First, I asked for hills. Then I asked for a loop.

> My drawing was rough, but it showed the idea better than perfect words.

> The computer helped turn that drawing into a rule. Now every point had a
> place to go.

### 0:36–0:58 — Milestone 2: the sketch becomes a Surface

> Next, I wanted the track to spiral down in three dimensions, like a funnel.

> A line was not wide enough for a track, so we gave it a second direction.

> The letter U moves along the ride. The letter V moves across its width.

> A number called phi helps each turn become smaller than the one before it.

> At the end, the height, width, and circle all squeeze toward zero. That gives
> the track one exact finish.

> The formula was starting to look like a roller coaster. But it was still only
> a shape.

### 0:58–1:15 — Milestone 3: the Surface becomes a ride

> So I gave one last instruction: make it a ride.

> Code gave the Surface rails, a starting point, a camera, and a way to move.

> Then the computer checked more than a thousand things, so the track, the
> room, and the camera would agree.

> The drawing had become math. The math had become code. Now the code was ready
> to become an experience.

### 1:15–1:34 — Reader and gameplay

> Before riding, we can stop and read the idea behind the track.

> This is the same Surface we just watched grow from the sketch. It is not a
> picture placed on the ride. It is the ride.

> Ready? Let’s follow the formula.

> The first turns are wide and easy to see.

> Each turn becomes smaller, and the track keeps falling toward its final
> point.

> Near the center, the track is too tiny for an ordinary coaster car. The
> camera keeps following the mathematical path.

> And at zero, the ride disappears into the black hole.

Let the final black frame end in silence. If a separate closing card is added
later, the optional closing line is:

> A question became a sketch. A sketch became a formula. And a formula became
> a world we could ride.

## Narration notes

- Say `phi` like “fye.”
- Say “Surface of U and V”; do not read every symbol in the full equation.
- Do not read the technical word cloud aloud.
- Keep the ride narration sparse so the movement has room to breathe.
- Sound curious and pleased, not like a commercial.
