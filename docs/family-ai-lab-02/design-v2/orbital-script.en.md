# How Can 0 and 1 Send a Message?


## fa02-01-01

The note won't fit! How can Mia invite Leo to play?


## fa02-01-02

Their space pods are linked by one tiny pipe.


## fa02-01-03

One little ball goes through at a time. Gold means one. Blue means zero.


## fa02-01-04

Could you send a message with only those two kinds of signal?


## fa02-02-01

Start with one little ball. It has just two possible values.


## fa02-02-02

Put two in a row, and there are four different patterns. Their order matters.


## fa02-02-03

Each zero or one is called a bit. Now put eight bits in a group.


## fa02-02-04

That group is a byte. It can have two hundred and fifty-six different patterns.


## fa02-03-01

Before the game, they agreed on the same code table. Both ends must read it the same way.


## fa02-03-02

Reading from right to left, each place is worth twice as much: one, two, four, and so on.


## fa02-03-03

Use the sixty-four and one places. That makes sixty-five: the letter A in their table.


## fa02-03-04

Send the leading zeros too. They agreed to read a letter after all eight balls arrive.


## fa02-03-05

Here they use ASCII letters written with eight bits. Another code table could use different rules.


## fa02-04-01

Mia sends her invitation. The first letter is P. She sends its pattern, one ball at a time.


## fa02-04-02

Then come L, A and Y. Every eight balls give Leo another letter on his screen.


## fa02-04-03

The letters spell PLAY. The note stayed behind, but the invitation got through.


## fa02-05-01

Before the game, Mia hid a little gift in one of the ten boxes in Leo's cargo bay.


## fa02-05-02

They agreed on a set of yes-or-no questions. Leo follows it. Mia replies with a gold or blue ball.


## fa02-05-03

Is it box one? Box two? If the answers are no, almost all the boxes remain.


## fa02-05-04

Try this: is the number greater than five? Yes. Five boxes disappear at once.


## fa02-05-05

Greater than eight? No. Only six, seven and eight remain.


## fa02-05-06

Greater than six? Yes. That leaves seven and eight.


## fa02-05-07

Greater than seven? No. There it is: box seven!


## fa02-06-01

Three yes-or-no answers can distinguish at most eight outcomes. That cannot cover ten boxes.


## fa02-06-02

Four answers allow sixteen outcomes. With suitable questions, four always suffice for any of the ten boxes.


## fa02-06-03

Even a thousand possibilities need only ten answers, if each question splits the remaining choices as evenly as possible.


## fa02-07-01

The box is found. Which snack is inside: a banana, an apple, grapes, or watermelon?


## fa02-07-02

Is it red in these pictures? Yes. Put the banana and grapes aside.


## fa02-07-03

Can you see seeds? Yes. It is the watermelon slice. Two questions found it.


## fa02-07-04

Two questions work here because the questions separate the choices. Any two questions will not necessarily do.


## fa02-08-01

Leo presses Send. A gold signal carries his reply: got it!


## fa02-08-02

The balls carry our message. A light can send the same signals: off for zero, on for one.


## fa02-08-03

In the online lab, switch on sixty-four and one. See whether the letter A appears.


## fa02-08-04

Or print the table. Write a message for your child to decode, then let them make one for you.


## fa02-08-05

Today we tried English letters. Eight bits make a byte, but not every character fits in one byte.
