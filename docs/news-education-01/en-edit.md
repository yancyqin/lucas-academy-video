## SC00 · France, this month

- sc00-01 | In October 2026, France saw its biggest riots in years. What are people protesting about?
- sc00-02 | It started at one high school. In September 2026, at a high school outside Paris, the teachers went on strike. The reason was simple: there were not enough teachers, and several classes had no one to teach them.
- sc00-03 | The next day, students blocked the school gate. Within three weeks, more than a thousand high schools across France were caught up in it, and later university students joined in.
- sc00-04 | Their reasons are real: more than thirty students in a class, classrooms far too hot, missing teachers who are never replaced, and many graduates anxious about getting into university.
- sc00-05 | By October, people protesting the cost of living had joined them. On October 17, the Interior Ministry counted 550,000 people in the streets.
- sc00-06 | But it went further and further. Twenty-four high schools were burned or wrecked, and thirty thousand students could not go to class. More than a hundred and forty teachers and school staff were hurt.
- sc00-07 | On the other side, nearly eight thousand people were detained, most of them minors. A fifteen-year-old lost a hand to a police grenade in the clashes. Parts of Paris and Lyon went under curfew.
- sc00-08 | The students have their reasons, and so does the government. But the harder both sides push, the more people get hurt. And what burns is the students' own schools.
- sc00-09 | The French have taken to the streets. So where is the way out?

## SC01 · Not only France

- sc01-01 | In recent years, from Bangladesh and Nepal to Indonesia, the Philippines and India, young people in Asia have taken to the streets again and again.
- sc01-02 | The sparks were different: in one place, housing allowances for lawmakers; in another, the children of the powerful flaunting their wealth; in others, corruption in flood-control projects, leaked exam papers, or a ban on social media. But the roots look alike: corruption, privilege, and young people who cannot see a future.
- sc01-03 | Some changes came fast. In 2024, students in Bangladesh took to the streets. A prime minister who had ruled for fifteen years fell, and about 1,400 people died.
- sc01-04 | In September 2025, young people in Nepal took to the streets, and more than seventy people died. In less than two days, the prime minister resigned. Six months later, a party that had been small won two-thirds of the seats in parliament.
- sc01-05 | But after the people at the top changed, what then?
- sc01-06 | Since 1991, Nepal has had twenty-seven prime ministers. Not one has served a full five-year term, and often the same few people take turns.
- sc01-07 | In Madagascar, a colonel sided with the young protesters and took power. A few months later, young people who took to the streets were being arrested again.
- sc01-08 | Even Nepal's new government, with a majority in parliament, often goes around parliament and pushes reforms through by decree.
- sc01-09 | Protest can change who holds power. It is much harder to change how power is held.

## SC02 · A pile of solutions

- sc02-01 | So what should be done? Ask AI, and it will list a pile of solutions in one breath: more jobs, an anti-corruption commission, a voice for young people in decisions...
- sc02-02 | Governments have plans too. France announced three thousand substitute teachers, and is pushing a law to make people who wreck schools pay, up to ten thousand euros each. Indonesia cancelled the lawmakers' housing allowance.
- sc02-03 | Every one of these makes sense, but they are all patches. France has been short of teachers for years. In many places, the problems have been building for a long time, and no single plan can solve them.
- sc02-04 | So in this episode, I am not going to talk about putting out the fire in front of us. I want to talk about how the next generation can start fewer fires: preventing them before they begin.

## SC03 · Every side has a point

- sc03-01 | First, let us separate two words: rights, and power.
- sc03-02 | The first is rights: what every person has from the start, to speak, to question, to protest. The second is power: the strength to govern others, to keep order, to make rules, to punish.
- sc03-03 | The French students have the right to protest. The government has the power to keep order. Neither side is wrong.
- sc03-04 | But both sides use their rights and their power to the limit. Blocking school gates turned into burning schools and hurting teachers. Keeping order turned into grenades, curfews, and making parents pay. Every step makes sense; added together, it is a slide downhill, and it keeps getting worse.
- sc03-05 | The way out is not to take away the students' rights, and it is not about who wins. Because whoever wins, the tension is still there.
- sc03-06 | I think the way out lies in another direction: both sides lay down the power they think they have.
- sc03-07 | Laying it down is not giving in, and it is not giving up what is right. What you lay down is exactly what is yours, what you could justify: to have it, and not insist on it; to be able to use it, and not use it to the full. To care more about the other side's rights, and the other side's feelings.
- sc03-08 | That sounds hard. But in history, many people have done it.

## SC04 · Power that limits power

- sc04-01 | First, a little background. More than two hundred years ago, there were thirteen British colonies in North America. In 1775 they rose up against the British king and fought an eight-year war for independence. Their commander-in-chief was George Washington.
- sc04-02 | In 1782, the war was nearly over. But the new country was poor. The central government could not collect taxes, and the soldiers had gone unpaid for a long time. Many in the army felt that a government like this could not be trusted.
- sc04-03 | A colonel wrote to Washington, suggesting that America should perhaps have a king, and that the best man for it was Washington himself. Washington wrote back that he viewed the idea with abhorrence, and asked him to banish it from his mind and never mention it to anyone again.
- sc04-04 | The next spring, at the army camp in Newburgh, New York, the pay still had not come. Someone wrote an anonymous letter urging the officers to use the army in their hands to put pressure on Congress.
- sc04-05 | Washington called the officers together and urged them not to do it. Then he began to read a letter from a member of Congress. After a few lines, he stopped and took a pair of glasses from his pocket: "Gentlemen, you will permit me to put on my spectacles, for I have not only grown gray but almost blind in the service of my country."
- sc04-06 | Many officers wept on the spot. They voted to trust Congress, and the mutiny simply melted away.
- sc04-07 | That December, with the war over, Washington went before Congress, handed back his commission as commander-in-chief, and went home to his farm. It is said that when the British king heard he would go home after the war, he said: if he does that, he will be the greatest man in the world.
- sc04-08 | He had an army, he had respect, and there were people begging him to be king. He had it, and he did not use it.
- sc04-09 | But one good man is not enough. In the first years after independence, the central government was so weak it could not even pay the army. Yet if the government grew too strong, people feared it would become a new king.
- sc04-10 | In 1787, the states sent delegates to Philadelphia. After four months of meetings, they wrote the Constitution of the United States. Their answer was to split power into three: Congress makes the laws, the President carries them out, and the courts judge cases by the law.
- sc04-11 | The three check one another. The President can veto bills from Congress. Congress can impeach the President. The courts can rule that a law violates the Constitution. No single branch gets the final say.
- sc04-12 | Once the Constitution was written, the states still had to agree before it took effect. James Madison, who helped draft it, wrote a series of essays with two others in New York newspapers, explaining to ordinary people why it was designed this way. They were later collected in a book called The Federalist.
- sc04-13 | In number fifty-one, Madison first says that the surest way to keep power from slowly gathering in one branch is to give the people who run each branch both the constitutional means and their own motives to resist the others reaching in.
- sc04-14 | Then he writes: "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition. It may be a reflection on human nature, that such devices should be necessary to control the abuses of government. But what is government itself, but the greatest of all reflections on human nature?"
- sc04-15 | "If men were angels, no government would be necessary. If angels were to govern men, neither external nor internal controls on government would be necessary."
- sc04-16 | "In framing a government which is to be administered by men over men, the great difficulty lies in this: you must first enable the government to control the governed; and in the next place oblige it to control itself. A dependence on the people is, no doubt, the primary control on the government; but experience has taught mankind the necessity of auxiliary precautions."
- sc04-17 | In plain words: a government needs strength to keep order. But the people in power are people, not angels, so the government itself must also be kept in check. That is power that limits power.
- sc04-18 | In 1791, ten amendments were added to the Constitution, called the Bill of Rights. The first one says that Congress shall make no law abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press, or the right of the people peaceably to assemble, and to petition the Government.
- sc04-19 | Notice the word "peaceably." Going into the streets to speak out is a right the Constitution protects. But it is the right to assemble peaceably.
- sc04-20 | Washington became the first President of the United States. After two terms, he stepped down once again, on his own. For more than a hundred years after that, no American president served more than two terms in a row; later, the tradition was written into the Constitution.
- sc04-21 | Institutions limit power. People choose to lay power down. We need both.

## SC05 · The Puritans

- sc05-01 | This idea, that power must always be limited, has several sources. One of them goes back more than a hundred years earlier: the Puritans.
- sc05-02 | They left England for their faith and came to North America. They respected the Bible, and they believed that everyone is corrupted by sin, including those in power.
- sc05-03 | In 1638, the pastor Thomas Hooker preached that the foundation of authority lies in the free consent of the people, and that those who have the power to appoint officials also have the power to set the bounds of their power.
- sc05-04 | The pastor John Cotton put it even more bluntly: all power on earth must be limited, the church's power included.
- sc05-05 | In 1647, Massachusetts passed a law: every town of fifty households had to hire a teacher to teach the children to read and write, so that everyone could read the Bible for themselves, and the key of knowledge would not stay in the hands of a few. Many people see it as the beginning of public schools in America.
- sc05-06 | The Puritans were not perfect. Once in power, they too drove out people who disagreed with them, and put Quakers to death. Which shows exactly this: even people who respect the Bible must have their power limited.

## SC06 · The Bible

- sc06-01 | So what does the Bible say about power?
- sc06-02 | At the Last Supper, Jesus got up, took off his outer garment, wrapped a towel around his waist, poured water into a basin, and washed his disciples' feet.
- sc06-03 | When he had finished, he said: "You call me, 'Teacher' and 'Lord.' You say so correctly, for so I am. If I then, the Lord and the Teacher, have washed your feet, you also ought to wash one another's feet."
- sc06-04 | His authority was real, and he did not deny it. But he bent down and did a servant's work.
- sc06-05 | A few days earlier, he had said: "Unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains by itself alone. But if it dies, it bears much fruit."
- sc06-06 | What the grain lays down is its own life. Laying it down is not losing it; only when it is laid down does it bear fruit.

## SC07 · Gandhi and King

- sc07-01 | This spirit is not found only in America.
- sc07-02 | As a young man, Gandhi read the Sermon on the Mount, and said it went straight to his heart. Later he led Indians in nonviolent non-cooperation to win independence.
- sc07-03 | In 1920 he wrote: "I believe that non-violence is infinitely superior to violence. But abstinence is forgiveness only when there is the power to punish. A mouse hardly forgives a cat when it allows itself to be torn to pieces by her."
- sc07-04 | In January 1956, Martin Luther King Jr.'s house was bombed, with his wife and their two-month-old daughter inside. An angry crowd gathered outside, and some of them carried guns.
- sc07-05 | He stood on his porch and said: if you have weapons, take them home; we must meet violence with nonviolence.
- sc07-06 | Later he wrote: Christ furnished the spirit and motivation, while Gandhi furnished the method.
- sc07-07 | It was not that they had no strength, or no reason. They had it, and they did not use it.

## SC08 · The classroom

- sc08-01 | So what does this have to do with education?
- sc08-02 | The first time we hold power, and the first time we run into someone else's power, is not in the street. It is at home, and in the classroom.
- sc08-03 | Teachers have power: to assign homework, to call parents in, to keep students after school.
- sc08-04 | Students have rights too: the right not to like a teacher, the right not to like what they are learning, the right not to like the school lunch.
- sc08-05 | This leads straight to an idea that matters a great deal in American education: critical literacy. Not only understanding what a text says, but reading why it is there: who chose it, for whom, and what was left out.
- sc08-06 | Say the textbook says Columbus discovered America. A student raises a hand and asks: what about the people who already lived there? They were there long before.
- sc08-07 | So when students ask why, question the homework, and question what they learn, it is not just their right. It is something learning should grow in them.
- sc08-08 | But if the teacher treats the question as defiance and calls the parents, and the student uses that right to humiliate the teacher in front of the class, each side insisting only on its own power, the classroom stops being a place of mutual respect.
- sc08-09 | At Lucas Academy, our rule is: low demands on content, high demands on relationships.
- sc08-10 | On content, children can ask any question, disagree with the teacher, and say a lesson was badly designed. The teacher first asks, "Why do you think so?" rather than reaching for a punishment.
- sc08-11 | On relationships, there is only one rule, but it asks a lot: love your neighbor as yourself, and respect one another. That one rule covers almost everything.
- sc08-12 | Teachers have power, and do not reach for it first. Students have rights, and when they use them, they care about the teacher's feelings too.

## SC09 · On the ski slope

- sc09-01 | And here I have to tell you about something I did not do well myself.
- sc09-02 | Once, while we were skiing, a man crashed into my child, then turned and fled.
- sc09-03 | I chased him. He tried to shake me off and could not. I caught up with him twice, shouted at him to stop, and would not let him go. He was scared. In that moment, I even wanted to hit him.
- sc09-04 | I had right on my side: he hit someone, and he ran. I had strength on my side too: I ski faster than he does.
- sc09-05 | My nine-year-old, Matthew, said something to me, something like: violence cannot fix violence.
- sc09-06 | Once I was sure my child was not hurt, I let the man go.
- sc09-07 | That day, I was not a good example. But in my nine-year-old, I saw hope.

## SC10 · Protecting kindness

- sc10-01 | But kindness is often taken for weakness, and taken advantage of. Hitting someone and running away is taking advantage of other people's kindness.
- sc10-02 | So homes, schools and society all need to protect that kindness, so that it does not lose out.
- sc10-03 | The responsibility code at American ski areas says it plainly: if you are involved in or see a collision, share your contact information with each other and with a ski area employee.
- sc10-04 | With a rule like that, a child's kindness does not lose out, and adults do not need their fists to get justice.
- sc10-05 | That is power that limits power: institutions protect the people who lay power down, and the people who lay it down bring the institutions to life.
- sc10-06 | What education must do is plant this grain of wheat in a child's heart, and stand beside the child, protecting it as it grows.
- sc10-07 | It is slow. A grain of wheat takes a long time to bear fruit. But the people who take to the streets ten years from now are the children in our classrooms today.

## SC11 · The question

- sc11-01 | Next time, at home, in the classroom, or in the street, when you have right on your side and strength in your hands, will you lay down just a little first?
- sc11-02 | That is also the question we learn and think about together, at Lucas Academy.
