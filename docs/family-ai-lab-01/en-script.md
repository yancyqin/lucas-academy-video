# Family AI Lab 1 · English review script

Louise narration script. One common picture, English default audio. Original experiment values only.

## S1 · The same picture

- **fa01-01-01** Let’s take Claude Fable 5.1 and GPT-6-Astra back to 1957 for an AI speed race!
- **fa01-01-02** How fast can a computer recognize a seven?
- **fa01-01-03** Give the same seven to four programs, and watch who answers first.
- **fa01-01-04** Ready? Let's start their clocks together and watch what happens.
- **fa01-01-05** X has already finished. Its time is 0.37 microseconds per picture.
- **fa01-01-06** These are the original result files saved from the experiment.
- **fa01-01-07** Across ten pictures it had never trained on, X got every answer right.
- **fa01-01-08** So who is this mysterious X, with its name still hidden from us?

## S2 · The speed headline

- **fa01-02-01** 3 million times faster than Claude Fable 5.1, and 10 million times faster than GPT-6-Astra!

## S3 · Paper and pencil

- **fa01-03-01** Let’s return to our family AI classroom. First, get some paper and pencils.
- **fa01-03-02** A computer has no eyes like ours. How can we give it a picture?
- **fa01-03-03** Divide the picture into little squares, and keep just one color in each square.
- **fa01-03-04** Make it simpler still: write one for black, and zero for white.
- **fa01-03-05** Read a row of numbers aloud. Let your child color the squares to match.
- **fa01-03-06** Keep the picture a secret for a moment. See when your child recognizes it.
- **fa01-03-07** Now try this cat. Here is the photograph before we turn it into squares.
- **fa01-03-08** As the squares get bigger, the fur and whiskers become harder to make out.
- **fa01-03-09** Eventually there are only eight rows left. Can you still spot the cat's ears?
- **fa01-03-10** Each row has eight zeros and ones. That fits neatly inside one byte.
- **fa01-03-11** So the whole tiny cat takes just eight bytes to store.
- **fa01-03-12** Send the numbers to a friend. They can recreate the picture on paper.

## S4 · Be a neuron

- **fa01-04-01** Your eyes can see the colored squares. How do you work out what the picture is?
- **fa01-04-02** Let's borrow a very simple idea from a neuron in the brain.
- **fa01-04-03** A neuron receives many signals. Some connections have a stronger effect than others.
- **fa01-04-04** When the combined input is enough, it sends a signal of its own.
- **fa01-04-05** The human brain is often estimated to contain about eighty-six billion neurons.
- **fa01-04-06** We can start with a much simpler model, and turn it into a game.
- **fa01-04-07** Pretend you are a neuron. Your job is to decide whether something is an apple.
- **fa01-04-08** Give it one point for being red, and another point for being round.
- **fa01-04-09** When it gets two points, raise your hand. That means you think you found one.
- **fa01-04-10** But a tomato can collect both points too. What could you change?
- **fa01-04-11** Try another clue: does it taste sweet? How much should that clue count?
- **fa01-04-12** Back in 1949, Hebb proposed that repeated activity together could strengthen connections.

## S5 · The browser lab

- **fa01-05-01** Now let’s move the points game into our Family AI Lab webpage.
- **fa01-05-02** Every square in the picture has its own points value, ready to be adjusted.
- **fa01-05-03** Click Run one round, and watch the points while it works through the examples.
- **fa01-05-04** If a horizontal line should switch the light on and does not, add points.
- **fa01-05-05** Only change the points for squares that are black in this particular picture.
- **fa01-05-06** Look underneath the grid. The numbers in the points list change at the same time.
- **fa01-05-07** If the light comes on when it should stay off, lower those points instead.
- **fa01-05-08** Now switch to a twenty-by-twenty picture. The learning rule stays just the same.
- **fa01-05-09** After training, do the blue points begin to remind you of a seven?
- **fa01-05-10** The predict function adds up the points belonging to the black squares.
- **fa01-05-11** The train function checks the answer and decides whether those points need changing.
- **fa01-05-12** We supply examples and answers. The program adjusts the individual points as it learns.

## S6 · Meet X

- **fa01-06-01** Now we can reveal X. It is a perceptron.
- **fa01-06-02** Frank Rosenblatt described his perceptron ideas in a report published in 1957.
- **fa01-06-03** The later Mark I machine had an eye made from a grid of light sensors.
- **fa01-06-04** Those sensors were arranged in a twenty-by-twenty array, reading light and dark from a card.
- **fa01-06-05** To adjust a connection's strength, a small motor physically turned a potentiometer.
- **fa01-06-06** After learning, another card could be presented, and the output indicated the machine's decision.
- **fa01-06-07** The positions of those adjustable controls physically stored what the machine had learned.
- **fa01-06-08** A 1958 newspaper headline described a new Navy device that learns by doing.
- **fa01-06-09** This race used a simplified perceptron rule written in JavaScript and running on this Mac.
- **fa01-06-10** It had already learned from 419 training pictures before entering the race.

## S7 · What came next

- **fa01-07-01** Why was it so fast? Now we can look at how much work it actually does.
- **fa01-07-02** For one picture, it needs at most 400 additions to collect the points.
- **fa01-07-03** The local Qwen model has roughly eight billion parameters, a much larger collection of numbers.
- **fa01-07-04** It calculates across many layers, then writes its answer one token at a time.
- **fa01-07-05** This simple artificial neuron was part of a much longer story of increasingly complex networks.
- **fa01-07-06** In 1969, Minsky and Papert published a detailed mathematical study of perceptrons and their limitations.
- **fa01-07-07** Interest later cooled for a time. Backpropagation helped make networks with multiple layers learn effectively.
- **fa01-07-08** In 2012, a deep neural network made a major breakthrough in the ImageNet picture-recognition competition.
- **fa01-07-09** Then came the Transformer, followed by the conversational AI systems we use today.
- **fa01-07-10** Those models can discuss far more than this one small question about a picture.
- **fa01-07-11** Even a simple request can still require a great deal of computation.
- **fa01-07-12** For example, the local Qwen model got only five pictures right in this experiment.
