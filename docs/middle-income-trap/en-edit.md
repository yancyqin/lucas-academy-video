- sc00-02 | And it's not only countries. A person, a family, can hit this kind of ceiling too. We'll start with countries, and end at home: what each of us can do, and how we grow together with our children. You can also use the progress bar to jump ahead.

- sc00-04 | It means this: many countries climb out of poverty to middle income, and then stop rising.
- sc00-05 | So what keeps a middle-income country stuck, unable to break through that ceiling?
- sc00-06 | Is it supply? Demand? Or institutions?
- sc00-07 | Let's hear Li Lu first.

## SC01 · The Evidence

- sc01-01 | First, does the evidence say education is necessary to break through the middle-income ceiling?
- sc01-02 | Start with individuals. Many believe that moving up, for a person, a family, even a whole class, depends a lot on education. That's why families invest so much in it.
- sc01-03 | It's not just a feeling. Two World Bank economists pooled 1,120 studies from 139 countries: one more year of school raises income by about 9%, and that has held for decades.
- sc01-04 | What about whole countries? Yes, and no.
- sc01-05 | Yes, because the countries and regions that broke through all put education ahead of income. While South Korea, Taiwan and Ireland were still middle-income, high school among their workers was already close to rich-country levels.
- sc01-06 | Economists studying slowdowns in dozens of countries also found: the more educated the people, the less likely growth stalls at middle income.
- sc01-07 | And no, because schooling alone isn't enough. Many Latin American countries have plenty of years in school, yet their economies stayed stuck. Economists call it the Latin American growth puzzle.
- sc01-08 | Hanushek at Stanford and Woessmann in Germany measured differently: not years in school, but what students actually learned on tests.
- sc01-09 | Measured that way, the puzzle is solved. Latin American students score near the bottom, and that alone explains much of the slow growth.
- sc01-10 | So the evidence says: spending on education is necessary, but not enough. Quality matters more.

## SC02 · What Is Useful to Learn?

- sc02-01 | So what is a high-quality education? And what is useful to learn now?
- sc02-02 | Electrical engineering? Computer science? Economics? Math? Art? Business? Whatever we pick, we worry AI will replace it.
- sc02-03 | What do ordinary people do? The rat race.
- sc02-04 | Primary school, middle school, high school, college: race all the way, top universities, then a master's, a PhD.
- sc02-05 | Not a bad path, but a costly one.
- sc02-06 | And that's only the cost you can see. Harder to see is the opportunity cost.
- sc02-07 | If you're curious, tap the top-right corner for our other video.
- sc02-08 | All in all, if every family in a country runs this race, family savings all pour into the "supply side," to produce standardized talent.
- sc02-09 | Why do people who learn different things all end up standardized? Go back to the question we started with: "What is useful to learn now?"
- sc02-10 | Did you notice? Following that question, we naturally start thinking: what should I learn so it pays off?
- sc02-11 | When everyone thinks this way, a whole country produces people by one standard: usefulness.
- sc02-12 | But the more standardized something is, the easier AI can replace it.
- sc02-13 | Li Lu made this point too.

> Clip B (46 s): Li Lu's own words.

- sc02-14 | In short: zero-sum games push us toward the short term. What you learn today may be useless tomorrow, so nothing builds up.
- sc02-15 | We lean more and more on machines, then on AI, until one day we're replaced. So as a person, where is your value?
- sc02-16 | His answer: keep learning, and let knowledge compound.
- sc02-17 | Let me be clear: I'm not saying stop learning languages, math, a second language, computing, or art. I'm asking: what is learning them for? What matters most?
- sc02-18 | If the main point of learning is job skills, AI already outdoes many people in language, math and coding, in making beautiful pictures, videos and music. So why learn job skills at all?
- sc02-19 | Hardly anyone learns to knit for a living anymore, right?
- sc02-20 | So in this new age, what is learning for? The process itself: making knowledge, skill and character truly your own.
- sc02-21 | In his 1936 talk "On Education," Einstein quoted, and agreed with, this saying: "Education is that which remains, if one has forgotten everything he learned in school."

## SC03 · Learning Needn't Be Useful

- sc03-01 | Back to what's useful to learn. Have you ever thought that learning doesn't have to be useful at all?
- sc03-02 | A student learned his first theorem and asked Euclid, "What will I get from this?" Euclid told his servant, "Give him threepence, since he must make gain out of what he learns."
- sc03-03 | Feynman, once burned out, played with physics just for fun and worked out how a tossed plate wobbles. His Nobel Prize work, he said, came from "piddling around with the wobbling plate."
- sc03-04 | In 1940, the mathematician Hardy wrote that real mathematics is "harmless and innocent": number theory had no use in war.
- sc03-05 | Less than forty years later, number theory became the basis of public-key encryption. It protects every payment we make online.
- sc03-06 | Zhuangzi said, two thousand years ago: "All know the use of the useful, none the use of the useless."
- sc03-07 | I'm not saying "useless" questions always turn out useful, though they often do. I'm saying the people who asked them weren't asking for a reward.

## SC04 · What Is Truly Useful?

- sc04-01 | So is learning to be useful a bad thing? Of course not. The question is: what counts as "useful"?
- sc04-02 | When I asked what's useful to learn, could you have challenged my question, and said learning needn't be useful?
- sc04-03 | Is that kind of questioning good? Is it useful? Very. It's a key idea in education: critical literacy.
- sc04-04 | Critical literacy means reading not only what a text says, but why it is there: who chose it, for whom, and what was left out.
- sc04-05 | It comes from the Brazilian educator Paulo Freire: read the word, and read the world.
- sc04-06 | And why are so many "useless" things useful? Because creativity and imagination rarely start from usefulness.
- sc04-07 | A child daydreaming isn't thinking about useful or useless. With good guidance, daydreams lead children to explore and discover, to create more freely and think more critically.
- sc04-08 | Psychologists gave people a creative task. During a break, one group did something easy that let their minds wander, the other something demanding. Back on the task, the mind-wanderers came up with clearly more ideas.
- sc04-09 | In another study, a teacher showed one function of a toy, and children found fewer other ways to play. Good guidance doesn't hand children the answer.
- sc04-10 | So quality education is an environment that draws out potential, where we learn from each other, keep learning and keep exploring, not just knowledge poured into heads.
- sc04-11 | Seen this way, learning only for what's useful, only to master job skills, seems obvious, yet it is exactly a trap. Chasing only results, we can lose something more precious without noticing: creativity, deep thinking, critical thinking, working together.

## SC05 · What Can Families Do?

- sc05-01 | So what can ordinary people, ordinary families do? Call for reform of the education system, change from the top down?
- sc05-02 | History says it can work. The civil rights movement led by Martin Luther King Jr., equal access to education, the end of segregation: they changed American education for the better, and in the end, society too.
- sc05-03 | America has largely left that kind of institutional segregation behind. It has new tensions and new challenges too, but that's another story.
- sc05-04 | And now in France, Gen Z has taken to the streets over education: classes of thirty-plus, classrooms too hot, missing teachers never replaced, and graduates anxious about getting into university.
- sc05-05 | We don't yet know what the protests will achieve, or their long-term effects. The point is, protests don't always work, or bring change from the top.
- sc05-06 | Is there another way for ordinary families? Yes: from the bottom up, starting with ourselves. Keep learning, explore together; learn with our children, and play with them.
- sc05-07 | Don't know how to teach? Learn. Don't know how to play? Try. Not sure what life is for? Talk honestly, think and explore together.
- sc05-08 | No big blocks of time needed. Measure and count while cooking, compare prices at the store, chat at bedtime, play a weekend board game. Ten or fifteen minutes a day is enough.
- sc05-09 | In one experiment, preschoolers from low-income families played a simple number board game, rolling a die and moving along squares 1 to 10, about fifteen minutes at a time, four times in all. They got better at counting, reading numbers and comparing amounts, and nine weeks later the gains remained.
- sc05-10 | In another, parents spent a few minutes at bedtime on short math story problems with their first graders. A year later, the children did better in math than the comparison group, more so when the parents feared math. Can't teach it? Learn beside them.
- sc05-11 | Video games aren't all bad. In Germany, adults played Nintendo's Super Mario 64 at least half an hour a day; after two months, brain areas for navigation and planning had more gray matter.
- sc05-12 | Oxford researchers, working with Nintendo, got players' real play time in Animal Crossing: New Horizons, and found those who played more were slightly happier.
- sc05-13 | We can sit down and play our kids' favorite games with them too, and afterward ask: how did you figure out that level?
- sc05-14 | Education like this can be as free as air and water.
- sc05-15 | That's why we do education: adults and children thinking and exploring together, changing step by step.
- sc05-16 | One year, two years, five years, ten: the ceilings over individuals, families and nations will eventually disappear.

## SC06 · "Eventually"

- sc06-01 | "Eventually" is a heavy word. Education and political stability often drive each other, and personal effort is often crushed by its times. Look at Argentina.
- sc06-02 | A century ago, Argentina was among the world's richest countries, richer per person than Germany or France.
- sc06-03 | As early as 1884, it passed a law for free, compulsory primary school, leading South America in education.
- sc06-04 | But compared with Australia and Canada, at similar incomes, Argentina's schooling lagged, and its regions lagged even more.
- sc06-05 | One economist compared Buenos Aires and Chicago in 1914. Both did the same business, shipping meat and grain from the interior, yet they took completely different paths.
- sc06-06 | The biggest difference was people's education, which shapes both the economy and whether a society holds together.
- sc06-07 | Decades of turmoil followed, and hurt education in turn. In 1966 the University of Buenos Aires was taken over, and about 300 professors left Argentina, over 200 of them scientists.
- sc06-08 | Teacher strikes were frequent. One study found children who lost primary school days to strikes earned less as adults.
- sc06-09 | These professors, these children, did nothing wrong.
- sc06-10 | Today, over 70% of Argentina's 15-year-olds fall short of basic math.

## SC07 · The Mustard Seed

- sc07-01 | In this episode, we started from Li Lu's question. The evidence: years in school aren't enough; what's learned matters more.
- sc07-02 | Then we asked what's useful to learn. If every family asks only "useful or not" and races on, we get standardized talent, and the more standard, the easier for AI to replace.
- sc07-03 | But learning needn't be useful. What's truly useful is questioning the question itself, curiosity, and creativity.
- sc07-04 | Top-down change doesn't always come. Families can start from the bottom up: learning and playing with our children.
- sc07-05 | And Argentina reminds us that "eventually" is a heavy word. Countries fall into the middle-income trap; learning has its own trap: asking only what's useful, looking only at results.
- sc07-06 | So, back to where we began: what keeps a country stuck beneath the ceiling?
- sc07-07 | I believe one big factor is the quality of education.
- sc07-08 | It isn't how much "useful" stuff children learn, but whether they still ask and try, and whether we adults learn and play beside them.
- sc07-09 | The affairs of nations are big. What we can do starts at home, today: keep curiosity alive, learn together, explore together.
- sc07-10 | Can our effort change the whole, or are we a mantis trying to stop a cart? Honestly, we don't know.
- sc07-11 | But we believe more and more children can hold on to this way of learning. The next 20, 30, 50, 100 years will belong to them and their children.
- sc07-12 | As the Bible says: "The Kingdom of Heaven is like a grain of mustard seed, which a man took, and sowed in his field; which indeed is smaller than all seeds. But when it is grown, it is greater than the herbs, and becomes a tree, so that the birds of the air come and lodge in its branches."
- sc07-13 | From the context, the Kingdom here means God's word. And where is God's word? The same context tells us: it is sown in people's hearts.
- sc07-14 | If we keep a little curiosity, wanting to know the world and truly understand others, this little word will surely sprout, take root, and grow into a great tree.
