# Why Do Pilots Say "Bravo"? English narration script (v0.5 · 2026-10-16 · approved by Yancy)

Fun Informatics 3. By Yancy Qin, Louise Yang | Lucas Academy. Plan: [FUN-INFORMATICS-03.md](../../FUN-INFORMATICS-03.md).

## NS00 · Opening: how do pilots say B?

- ns00-01 | Here's a puzzle. A pilot on the radio needs to say the letter B. What do they say?
  Picture: a big letter B
- ns00-02 | Not "bee." They say "Bravo." D is "Delta." T is "Tango."
  Picture: B, D, T flip into Bravo, Delta, Tango
- ns00-03 | And the number nine? Pilots say "niner."
- ns00-04 | Why not just say the letter? Why add an extra sound to nine? The answer is one word: <strong>noise</strong>.
  Picture: title card "Why Do Pilots Say 'Bravo'?" + Fun Informatics 3 + byline

## NS01 · B, or D?

- ns01-01 | Noise is anything that damages a message on its way. A loud classroom, a crackly phone call... what you said arrives with a little bit missing.
  Picture: a sentence flies from left to right; static eats a corner of it
- ns01-02 | Let's try it. I'll say one letter over a crackly radio. Listen. Which letter was that?
  Sound: radio static with one letter in it (B, its first sound lost in the noise)
- ns01-03 | B? D? Maybe E? Hmm… hard to tell, isn't it?
- ns01-04 | Say them slowly: B, D, E, P, T, V. They all end with the same long "ee." The only difference is the tiny sound at the start. Let the noise cover that tiny sound, and B turns into D.
  Picture: the six letters in a row, the shared "ee" lights up
- ns01-05 | Imagine a pilot hears D instead of B, and drives the plane down the wrong road at the airport.
- ns01-06 | Listen again. Same crackle, different word.
  Sound: the same static, this time "Bravo"
- ns01-07 | Bravo! Easy. Bravo and Delta don't sound alike at all. Even if the noise eats a piece, there's plenty left to tell them apart.
- ns01-08 | "Niner" works the same way: the extra sound helps nine stand out on a crackly radio.
- ns01-09 | That's the first trick for beating noise: <strong>if two things sound alike, say them differently.</strong>

## NS02 · Pictionary (filmed)

- ns02-01 | Drawings have noise too. We played two rounds of Pictionary. The person drawing can't talk or write. Only draw.
  Picture: the drawings from above (footage/, steadied, cleaned up, background blurred)
- ns02-02 | Round one: free draw. Draw anything you like, and the guessers can shout anything.
- ns02-03 | This one got guess after guess: "Egg?" "Alien?" "Baby?"... until finally: "Lucas!"
  Picture: footage/r1-lucas; each guess pops up as a bubble; the real voices when it's guessed
- ns02-04 | The drawing wasn't bad. A round face and two eyes could be almost anyone, so it looked like everything.
- ns02-05 | Same with the next one: "A triangle?" "A sheep?" "A dog?" Yes, a dog: our friend's corgi, Cashew!
  Picture: footage/r1-cachew; bubbles; the real voices when it's guessed
- ns02-06 | Round two: twin cards. Each card has two things that look alike, like umbrella and mushroom. Everyone can see both words. The person drawing flips a coin in secret to pick one.
  Picture: the orange twin card; the coin flips
- ns02-07 | This time there are only fifteen seconds, and when time's up, the guessers get just one guess.
  Picture: the three twin drawings side by side: umbrella, moon, star
- ns02-08 | Just a curved crescent? Then some say banana, some say moon: fifty-fifty, just like the coin.
  Picture: footage/r2-moon, close; "Banana?" "Moon?" bubbles rocking
- ns02-09 | But the drawers learned fast. Give the umbrella a thin handle, and it's guessed at once. Add a few tiny stars and streaks of light around the star, and everyone changes their mind: "Oh, it's a star in the sky!"
  Picture: footage/r2-umbrella, r2-star; the handle and the tiny stars circled
- ns02-10 | Those extra strokes are redundancy. Not just more drawing, but exactly the part that tells it apart. Same idea as Bravo.

## NS03 · Closing

- ns03-01 | Last time, we said redundancy is like a backup that helps a message survive noise. Today we saw one way it works: make the things that are easy to mix up different.
  Picture: two small cards: Bravo | the tiny stars around the star
- ns03-02 | And Claude Shannon proved something amazing: if the extra bits are added cleverly enough, a message can get through the noise with almost no mistakes.
- ns03-03 | But sometimes noise is sneakier: it changes one letter and doesn't let you notice. What then? Next time.
  (teaser for the next episode, optional)
